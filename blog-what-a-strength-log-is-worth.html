What a strength log is worth, and why most of it never gets written

A short value analysis of training data in the weight room: what lifters record today, what the gaps cost, and what published research says rep-level data can do.

01The status quo

  • Logging means stopping between sets to type numbers into a phone or a notebook.
  • A written log records what was lifted: load and reps.
  • What it can't record: rep speed, which reps slowed, and how close each set came to failure.

02What the gaps cost

  • Effort is a guess. Asked to predict reps left before failure, lifters missed by about one rep on average, and more often thought they were closer to failure than they were.[4]
  • Coaches guess too. 259 certified coaches watching sets on video missed reps left by 4.8 reps on average early in a set, and by 1.2 near failure.[5]
  • A tested max goes stale. In one study, 1RM rose 9.3% in six weeks of training, so loads set from an old test drift off target.[1]
  • Fatigue goes unseen. Rep slowdown is the measure that tracks fatigue, and a written log has no place for it.[2]

03The analysis: bar speed is the signal

  • Speed reads load. In bench press, bar speed tracked percent of 1RM almost exactly (R² = 0.98, 120 lifters). After six weeks of training, 1RM rose 9.3% and the speed at each percent held steady.[1]
  • Slowdown reads fatigue. How much a set slowed tracked blood lactate (r = 0.93 to 0.97) and loss of jump height (r = 0.91 to 0.97).[2]
  • Slowdown sets the dose. Lifters who ended squat sets at a 20% slowdown gained similar strength to those who went to 40%, with 40% fewer reps, and improved their jump more (9.5% vs 3.5%).[3]

05The finding

The measures are proven. Bar speed and set slowdown have been studied in university labs since at least 2010.[1-3] The gap is collection. A written log has no place for rep speed, and judging effort by feel or by eye misses by about a rep or more.[4,5] The measure that tracks fatigue never makes it into the record.
